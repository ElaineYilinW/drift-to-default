\title{Drift to Default: How Autoregressive Video Rollouts\\ Forget Their Opening}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Autoregressive video diffusion models generate long videos chunk by chunk and drift over tens of seconds. We measure
drift by intervention: we shift the brightness, colour or scene class of the same opening frame and measure how much of
the shift survives. In fifteen preregistered studies on seven open models (six built on the Wan2.1-1.3B architecture,
one on the 13B HunyuanVideo), rollouts \emph{relax}: global tone and scene content move from the opening toward a
default set by the conditioning, keeping a share $\alpha$ that depends on the model, the prompt and the horizon. Under
a generic city prompt, openings of six scene classes lose 39--93\% of their class evidence within 30\,s. What decides
the long-run share is how the opening is held. Attention to a key--value cache alone forgets it completely; an
attention sink holds a share that grows with its depth and hinges on its absolute rotary position: re-aligning the
sink's positions cuts persistence at 60\,s from 0.61 to 0.16 and removes periodic bursts in which the opening, including
its scene content, returns. Re-denoising overlapping frames holds nearly all of it over 20\,s until the overlap is
shrunk. A linear chunk recursion predicted the prompt's per-chunk pull (0.08); bit-exact cache replay measured 0.088
\ci{0.045}{0.149}, but the recursion holds only for about 20\,s and does not account for the positional effects. In an
exploratory analysis, drift separates into forgetting the opening and an offset of the default from where the same
prompt starts, near zero for models trained on long rollouts. A content-aware correction and a deeper sink both reduce
drift without training; the correction keeps more legitimate brightness changes in some prompts. We report all 42
preregistered primary tests, 15 of which failed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autoregressive video diffusion models generate a video chunk by chunk, each chunk conditioned on the model's own
earlier output~\cite{diffusionforcing,causvid,selfforcing}. This makes streaming and open-ended generation possible and
is the basis of interactive world models~\cite{gamengen,diamond,vista}. It also makes them drift: over tens of seconds
the picture darkens or saturates, loses texture, and its content wanders. A growing family of methods targets this
failure with rolling windows, attention sinks, re-aligned positions, longer self-rollouts and test-time
correction~\cite{longlive,rollingforcing,deepforcing,selfforcingpp,pathwise,freqforcing,lol}. Most report a few samples
per prompt and an aggregate quality score. What drift \emph{is}, in quantitative terms, is rarely measured.

We measure it by intervention. We hold an opening frame, shift its brightness, colour or scene content \emph{within}
a scene, let the model continue, and ask how much of the shift survives. The answer has the same form in every model
we tested: the rollout \emph{relaxes}. It moves from its opening toward a default state set by the conditioning, and
keeps a model-specific share $\alpha$ of where it started,
\begin{equation}
x_\infty \approx \alpha\,x_{\text{open}} + (1-\alpha)\,\mu .
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
Written this way the law is just a linear response, and much of the paper is about which parts of it carry content.
Three findings do.

\textbf{What survives in the long run depends on how the opening is held, not on autoregression as such.} Models
that read the opening only through a key--value cache forget it completely. An attention sink holds a share that grows
with its depth and hinges on its absolute rotary position, which also produces periodic bursts in which the opening
returns. Re-denoising overlapping frames holds nearly all of it over 20\,s until the overlap is shrunk.

\textbf{The default is set by the conditioning.} Lighting words move the default brightness by up to 27~\Lstar, and
scene words decide whether a snowy, desert or forest opening stays or becomes a city street within 30\,s. Which parts of
the conditioning carry tone differs between models.

\textbf{Drift has two separable parts} (exploratory): forgetting the opening, $(1-\alpha)(x_{\text{open}}-\mu)$, and an
offset of the default itself from where the same prompt starts, which is small in the two models trained for long
rollouts and 4--11~\Lstar{} in models trained on short clips.

A linear chunk recursion, $\alpha_\infty = w_s/(w_s+w_p)$ with per-chunk pulls of the sink and the prompt, predicted
$w_p\approx0.08$; bit-exact replay of the key--value cache measured 0.088. It describes the first 20\,s, not the
positional effects that follow. Training-free levers act on either term: an early-step tone correction, a deeper sink,
and lighting words calibrated to a model's measured defaults, each with its own cost.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A measurement protocol for long-horizon drift: held and perturbed openings, persistence $\alpha$ and default
$\mu$ for tone and scene content, paired seeds pinned to one GPU architecture, and preregistered analyses.
\item An empirical account of relaxation across seven open models (five Wan-based cache models, SkyReels-V2 and the
Matrix-Game-2.0 world model, all of the Wan2.1-1.3B architecture, plus the 13B HunyuanVideo-based FramePack), for tone
and scene content, at horizons up to 120\,s: what sets $\mu$, what sets the long-run share (how, and at which position,
the opening is held), and an exploratory decomposition of drift into forgetting and attractor offset.
\item A phenomenological recursion whose predicted prompt pull we preregistered and then measured by bit-exact replay,
with its limits.
\item Training-free levers matched to the two terms, compared on the same prompts and seeds, with their costs and failures.
\end{enumerate}
We preregistered fifteen studies with hashed analysis code and report all 42 primary tests, including the 15 that
failed (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). No model is trained; all code and per-video statistics are public.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Autoregressive and long video diffusion.} Diffusion Forcing~\cite{diffusionforcing} and History-Guided Video
Diffusion~\cite{historyguided} enable causal rollout with per-frame noise and guidance on the history. CausVid~\cite{causvid}
distils a bidirectional teacher~\cite{wan} into a few-step causal student with DMD~\cite{dmd}; Self
Forcing~\cite{selfforcing} and Self-Forcing++~\cite{selfforcingpp} train on the student's own (minute-long) rollouts.
LongLive~\cite{longlive}, Rolling Forcing~\cite{rollingforcing} and Deep Forcing~\cite{deepforcing} add attention sinks,
rolling joint denoising and deeper, re-aligned sinks; LoL~\cite{lol} traces ``sink collapse'' to positional periodicity.
Segment-wise models condition on overlapping frames (SkyReels-V2~\cite{skyreels}, StreamingT2V~\cite{streamingt2v},
MAGI-1~\cite{magi}), a temporal pyramid (Pyramid Flow~\cite{pyramidflow}) or packed history with anti-drifting sampling
(FramePack~\cite{framepack}); training-free schemes lengthen bidirectional models~\cite{fifo,freenoise,freelong,rollingdiffusion}.
These works propose mechanisms against drift; we measure what drift does and which mechanism changes which part of it.

\paragraph{Error accumulation, exposure bias and world models.} Sampling from one's own output causes exposure bias in
sequence models~\cite{scheduledsampling,dagger} and diffusion models~\cite{ning2023,ning2024}; error analyses of
autoregressive video diffusion~\cite{metaarvdm} bound how errors compound, and game and driving world
models~\cite{gamengen,diamond,vista,matrixgame2} add noise to context frames for robustness. For global statistics our
results suggest a complementary view: the rollout forgets its context and relaxes to the conditioning's default, whose
offset from the short-horizon distribution is a training-horizon effect. Global brightness biases of diffusion models
arise at the earliest denoising steps~\cite{lin2024,offsetnoise}, consistent with corrections acting only there.

\paragraph{Attention sinks, forgetting and correction.} Streaming language models keep the first tokens as an attention
sink~\cite{streamingllm,sinkemerge}, related to registers in vision transformers~\cite{registers}; in autoregressive
video the sink is usually the first chunk kept in the key--value cache. The closest analogue to our finding is
instruction drift in dialogs~\cite{instructiondrift}, where attention to the system prompt decays and behaviour reverts
to a default. Pathwise test-time correction~\cite{pathwise} and FreqForcing~\cite{freqforcing} anchor rollouts to early
frames or their low frequencies; our correction is a minimal member of this family. VBench~\cite{vbench} scores imaging
quality with MUSIQ~\cite{musiq}; we measure drift against each prompt's fresh-start distribution~\cite{dinov2} and by
intervention.

\section{Measuring drift by intervention}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Models.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the configurations. Self Forcing (five checkpoints), CausVid,
LongLive and Rolling Forcing are causal few-step students of Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B~\cite{wan} that read their past through a
key--value cache of 21 latent frames (LongLive: 12), optionally with an attention sink (the first chunk kept in the
cache). SkyReels-V2-DF uses the Wan2.1 VAE and a transformer of the same architecture and size, but extends videos in
97-frame segments that re-denoise the last 17 frames of the previous segment. Matrix-Game-2.0~\cite{matrixgame2} is a
Wan-architecture world model without a text branch, driven by keyboard and mouse actions. FramePack~\cite{framepack} is a 13B model fine-tuned from HunyuanVideo~\cite{hunyuan}; each
33-frame section sees the opening as a clean prefix frame and as a SigLIP~\cite{siglip} image embedding, plus
compressed history. All Wan-based configurations run in one harness that reproduces the official Self Forcing loop
bit for bit when no intervention is applied.

\paragraph{Held and perturbed openings.} An opening image is held still for the first chunk (9 frames, 0.56\,s at
16\,fps; SkyReels: 17 frames as a prefix video; FramePack: the input image) and continued under a prompt. Openings
are NAVSIM~\cite{navsim} front-camera frames or frames generated by the models themselves. To separate what the model
keeps from what the scene is, we shift the \emph{same} opening in CIELAB before encoding: \Lstar{} by $\pm8$ and
$\pm16$, $b^*$ by $\pm12$. For scene content, we instead generate openings of six scene classes and vary the prompt
(Section~\ref{sec:content}).

\paragraph{Persistence and default.} For a statistic $x$ (a per-frame CIELAB mean), the persistence at time $T$ is the
within-scene least-squares slope of the end value $\bar x(T)$, averaged over the window $[T-w, T)$ after the opening,
on the opening value $x_{\text{open}}$ (mean of the held frames),
\begin{equation}
\alpha(T) = \frac{\partial\, \bar x(T)}{\partial\, x_{\text{open}}}\Big|_{\text{scene}},
\qquad \mu(T) = \frac{b_0}{1-\alpha(T)},
\end{equation}
where $b_0$ is the fitted intercept, so $\mu$ is the level at which the end equals the opening (the zero-drift level).
The window is the last 2\,s at 31\,s (study 6), 5\,s at 20--30\,s (studies 8, 9, 11), 10\,s at 60\,s and at
30\,s in studies 12 and 15, and 1\,s for curves; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:matched} compares all models over the same
window, 15--20\,s. Averaging slopes over
24 scenes gives a scene-bootstrap interval. The fresh default is where prompt-only rollouts end.

\paragraph{Content persistence.} For scene content we score each frame with CLIP ViT-L/14~\cite{clip} as the log-odds
of the opening's class against ``a city street'', $z = s\,(\cos(f, t_{\text{class}}) - \cos(f, t_{\text{city}}))$, and
define $\kappa(T) = (z(T)-\mu_z)/(z_{\text{open}}-\mu_z)$ with $\mu_z$ the class score of fresh rollouts of the generic
prompt. $\kappa$ is the content analogue of $\alpha$: 1 if the class evidence is kept, 0 if the rollout looks like a
fresh generic rollout.

\paragraph{Drift metrics for prompt-only rollouts.} Where no opening is held, drift is measured against fresh 5-s
generations of the same prompt, in colour statistics ($D$) and DINOv2~\cite{dinov2} features ($K$; Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\paragraph{Pairing and preregistration.} With bf16 kernels, the same seed and code give bit-identical videos within a
GPU architecture but different videos across A40, A100/A800 and L40S/RTX~6000 Ada. Arms that are compared therefore
run in one job, which deletes and regenerates its outputs if a preempted job resumes on another architecture. (In our
first study this was not yet enforced; 62\% of its nominal pairs crossed architectures, which widens but does not bias
its intervals.) Each study's hypotheses, decision rules and analysis code were frozen by SHA-256 hash before its
videos were generated (study 1: before analysis, after generation had started); studies 3--15 were also
committed publicly first. Tests within a study are Holm-corrected. Table~\ref{tab:primary} lists every primary test.
