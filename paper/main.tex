\begin{abstract}
Autoregressive video diffusion models generate long videos chunk by chunk and drift over tens of seconds. We measure
drift by intervention: we shift the brightness or colour of a held opening frame within a scene, or start from
openings of different scene classes, and measure how much of the opening survives. In \pend{25} preregistered studies
on nine open models (1.3B to 14B parameters, three model families), rollouts \emph{relax}: global tone and scene
content move from the opening toward a default set by the conditioning, keeping a share $\alpha$ that depends on the
model, the prompt and the horizon. Under a generic city prompt, openings of six scene classes lose 39--93\% of their
class evidence within 30\,s. How much is kept depends on whether the opening stays in the model's context. Once it
leaves, it is forgotten, through a sliding key--value window at 1.3B and at 14B parameters, and after a delay of about
20\,s when overlapping frames are re-denoised. An opening that stays in the context keeps a share whose size depends on
the model: 0.2--0.5 of its tone at 60\,s for multi-frame sinks, anchors and clean prefixes \pend{[study 24: at
180\,s]}, while a single anchored latent frame keeps almost no tone (0.03--0.08) but, in the 14B model, a third of the
scene. Attention sinks left at their absolute rotary
positions keep more, much of it in bursts at the query--sink distances where an attention probe finds the sink most
attended; moving the sink moves the bursts, and a sink held at such a distance freezes the video on its opening. In an exploratory analysis, drift separates into forgetting the opening and an
offset of the default from where the same prompt starts, small in the two models trained on their own long rollouts. A
content-aware correction and a deeper sink reduce drift without training. We report all \pend{N} preregistered primary
tests, \pend{M} of which failed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autoregressive video diffusion models generate a video chunk by chunk, each chunk conditioned on the model's own
earlier output~\cite{diffusionforcing,causvid,selfforcing}. This makes streaming and open-ended generation possible and
is the basis of interactive world models~\cite{gamengen,diamond,vista}. It also makes them drift: over tens of seconds
the picture darkens or saturates, loses texture, and its content wanders. A growing family of methods targets this
failure with rolling windows, attention sinks, re-aligned positions, longer self-rollouts and test-time
correction~\cite{longlive,rollingforcing,deepforcing,selfforcingpp,pathwise,freqforcing,lol}. Most report a few samples
per prompt and an aggregate quality score. What drift \emph{is}, in quantitative terms, is rarely measured.

We measure it by intervention. We hold an opening frame, shift its brightness or colour \emph{within} a scene, let
the model continue, and ask how much of the shift survives; for scene content we start from openings of different
classes. In every model we tested the rollout \emph{relaxes}: it moves from its opening toward a default state set by
the conditioning and keeps a share $\alpha$ of where it started that depends on the model, the prompt and the horizon,
\begin{equation}
x(T) \approx \alpha(T)\,x_{\text{open}} + \bigl(1-\alpha(T)\bigr)\,\mu .
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
This is a linear-response summary, not a law: $\alpha$ and $\mu$ come from one regression. What it organises are three
findings, and tests of its assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:relax}).

\textbf{What survives depends on whether the opening stays in the context, not on model size} (Fig.~\ref{fig:hold}).
Once the opening leaves a sliding key--value window it is forgotten, in 1.3B models and in a 14B model alike;
re-denoising overlapping frames passes it forward and delays forgetting by about 20\,s. An opening that stays in the
context (a re-rotated attention sink, an anchor, a clean copy in every section, full history) keeps a share whose size
depends on the model \pend{[and that levels off / keeps falling to 180\,s]}, and tone and scene can be kept separately:
a one-frame anchor in the 14B model keeps the scene but not its tone, LongLive's sink the reverse. A sink left at its absolute rotary
positions keeps more, much of it in bursts in which the opening, including its scene, returns when the sink's
distance to the current chunk passes a peak of the attention it draws; moving the sink moves the bursts.

\textbf{For a given checkpoint, the default is set by the conditioning.} Lighting words move the default brightness
by up to 27~\Lstar, and scene words decide whether a snowy, desert or forest opening stays or becomes a city street
within 30\,s. Which parts of the conditioning carry tone and which carry the scene differs between models.

\textbf{Drift has two separable parts} (exploratory): forgetting the opening, $(1-\alpha)(x_{\text{open}}-\mu)$, and an
offset of the default itself from where the same prompt starts, which is small in the two models trained on their own
long rollouts and 4--20~\Lstar{} in the others we measured. Two training-free levers act on these terms: an early-step tone
correction and a deeper sink, each with its own cost; lighting words matched to a model's measured defaults helped on
one model only.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A measurement protocol for long-horizon drift: held and perturbed openings, persistence $\alpha$ and default
$\mu$ for tone and scene content, paired seeds pinned to one GPU architecture, and preregistered analyses.
\item An empirical account of relaxation across nine open models (six of the Wan2.1-1.3B architecture, the Wan2.1-14B
student Krea Realtime, the HunyuanVideo-based FramePack and MAGI-1), for tone and scene content, at horizons up to
\pend{180}\,s: what sets $\mu$, what sets the long-run share, and an exploratory decomposition of drift into
forgetting and attractor offset.
\item A positional account of attention-sink bursts: an attention probe predicts when they occur, moving the sink moves
them, and holding the sink at a peak distance freezes the video (study 25).
\item Training-free levers matched to the two terms, compared on the same prompts and seeds, with their costs and failures.
\end{enumerate}
We preregistered \pend{25} studies with hashed analysis code and report all \pend{N} primary tests, including the
\pend{M} that failed (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). No model is trained; all code and per-video statistics are public.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Autoregressive and long video diffusion.} Diffusion Forcing~\cite{diffusionforcing} and History-Guided Video
Diffusion~\cite{historyguided} enable causal rollout with per-frame noise and guidance on the history. CausVid~\cite{causvid}
distils a bidirectional teacher~\cite{wan} into a few-step causal student with DMD~\cite{dmd}; Self
Forcing~\cite{selfforcing} and Self-Forcing++~\cite{selfforcingpp} train on the student's own (minute-long) rollouts.
LongLive~\cite{longlive}, Rolling Forcing~\cite{rollingforcing} and Deep Forcing~\cite{deepforcing} add attention sinks,
rolling joint denoising and deeper, re-aligned sinks; LoL~\cite{lol} traces ``sink collapse'' to positional periodicity.
Segment-wise models condition on overlapping frames~\cite{skyreels,streamingt2v,magi}, a temporal
pyramid~\cite{pyramidflow} or packed history~\cite{framepack}; training-free schemes lengthen bidirectional
models~\cite{fifo,freenoise,freelong,rollingdiffusion}. These works propose mechanisms against drift; we measure what
drift does and which mechanism changes which part of it.

\paragraph{Error accumulation, sinks and correction.} Sampling from one's own output causes exposure bias in sequence
models~\cite{scheduledsampling,dagger} and diffusion models~\cite{ning2023,ning2024}; error analyses of autoregressive
video diffusion~\cite{metaarvdm} bound how errors compound, and world models~\cite{gamengen,diamond,vista,matrixgame2}
add noise to context frames for robustness. For global statistics our results suggest a complementary view: the rollout
forgets its context and relaxes to the conditioning's default. Global brightness biases of diffusion models arise at the
earliest denoising steps~\cite{lin2024,offsetnoise}. Streaming language models keep the first tokens as an attention
sink~\cite{streamingllm,sinkemerge}, related to registers in vision transformers~\cite{registers}; the closest analogue
to our finding is instruction drift in dialogs~\cite{instructiondrift}, where attention to the system prompt decays and
behaviour reverts to a default. Pathwise test-time correction~\cite{pathwise} and FreqForcing~\cite{freqforcing} anchor
rollouts to early frames or their low frequencies; our correction is a minimal member of this family. VBench~\cite{vbench}
scores imaging quality with MUSIQ~\cite{musiq}; we measure drift against each prompt's fresh-start
distribution~\cite{dinov2} and by intervention.

\section{Measuring drift by intervention}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Models.} We study nine open models (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:models}). Self Forcing (five checkpoints),
CausVid, LongLive and Rolling Forcing are causal few-step students of Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B~\cite{wan} that read their past
through a key--value cache of 21 latent frames (LongLive: 12), optionally with an attention sink (the first chunk kept
in the cache); Krea Realtime 14B~\cite{krea} is a student of the 14B model. SkyReels-V2-DF~\cite{skyreels} (same
architecture as the 1.3B model) extends videos in overlapping segments, Matrix-Game-2.0~\cite{matrixgame2} is a
Wan-architecture world model driven by actions, FramePack~\cite{framepack} (13B, from HunyuanVideo~\cite{hunyuan})
conditions every section on the opening, and MAGI-1~\cite{magi} (4.5B) has its own VAE and transformer;
Section~\ref{sec:mech} describes how each holds the opening. All Wan-1.3B cache configurations run in one harness that
reproduces the official Self Forcing loop bit for bit when no intervention is applied.

\paragraph{Held and perturbed openings.} An opening image is held still for the first chunk (9 frames, 0.56\,s at
16\,fps; SkyReels: 17 frames as a prefix video; FramePack and Krea: the input image; MAGI-1: a clean image prefix) and continued
under a prompt. Openings
are NAVSIM~\cite{navsim} front-camera frames or frames generated by the models themselves. To separate what the model
keeps from what the scene is, we shift the \emph{same} opening in CIELAB before encoding: \Lstar{} by $\pm8$ and
$\pm16$, $b^*$ by $\pm12$. For scene content, we instead generate openings of six scene classes and vary the prompt
(Section~\ref{sec:content}).

\paragraph{Persistence and default.} For a statistic $x$ (a per-frame CIELAB mean), the persistence at time $T$ is the
within-scene least-squares slope of the end value $\bar x(T)$, averaged over the window $[T-w, T)$ after the opening,
on the opening value $x_{\text{open}}$ (mean of the held frames),
\begin{equation}
\alpha(T) = \frac{\partial\, \bar x(T)}{\partial\, x_{\text{open}}}\Big|_{\text{scene}},
\qquad \mu(T) = \frac{b_0}{1-\alpha(T)},
\end{equation}
where $b_0$ is the fitted intercept, so $\mu$ is the level at which the end equals the opening (the zero-drift level).
The window is 10\,s for endpoints at 30\,s or later (2--5\,s in the earlier studies with shorter endpoints) and
1\,s for curves; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:matched} compares all models over one window, 15--20\,s. Averaging slopes
over scenes gives a scene-bootstrap interval. The fresh default is where prompt-only rollouts end.

\paragraph{Content persistence.} For scene content we score each frame with CLIP ViT-L/14~\cite{clip} as the log-odds
of the opening's class against ``a city street'', $z = s\,(\cos(f, t_{\text{class}}) - \cos(f, t_{\text{city}}))$, and
define $\kappa(T) = (z(T)-\mu_z)/(z_{\text{open}}-\mu_z)$ with $\mu_z$ the class score of fresh rollouts of the generic
prompt over the same window. $\kappa$ is the content analogue of $\alpha$: 1 if the class evidence is kept, 0 if the rollout looks like a
fresh generic rollout.

\paragraph{Drift metrics for prompt-only rollouts.} Where no opening is held, drift is measured against fresh 5-s
generations of the same prompt, in colour statistics ($D$) and DINOv2~\cite{dinov2} features ($K$; Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\paragraph{Pairing and preregistration.} With bf16 kernels, the same seed and code give bit-identical videos within a
GPU architecture but different videos across architectures, so arms that are compared run in one job, which
regenerates its outputs if a preempted job resumes on another architecture. Each study's hypotheses, decision rules
and analysis code were frozen by SHA-256 hash before its videos were generated (study 1: before analysis), and studies
3--\pend{25} were committed publicly first. Tests within a study are Holm-corrected; Table~\ref{tab:primary} lists
every primary test.
